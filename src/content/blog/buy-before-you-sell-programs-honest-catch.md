<p>Buy the next house first. Move once. Never keep the place show-ready with kids, a dog, and a full work week. Sell the old one after you're settled. If that sounds too good to be true, you're asking the right question.</p>

<p>Buy-before-you-sell programs (also called equity advance programs) are real, and for a lot of families who've <a href="/move-up-sellers">outgrown their home in the South Denver Metro</a>, they're the smoothest way to move. But they come with fees, fine print, and a few ways things can go sideways. A common thread in the negative reviews: someone didn't understand the money side before they signed.</p>

<p>This guide covers the five honest catches, what the fees really cost using a home at <a href="/neighborhoods/littleton">Littleton's</a> 2026 single-family median, and the six questions to ask before you sign anything. Jacob Stark is a South Denver Metro REALTOR® who also renovates and invests in homes, so he looks at these programs the way he'd look at his own money. If you're still deciding between buying first and selling first, start with the <a href="/blog/buy-or-sell-first-south-denver-move-up">five ways to move up, compared</a>.</p>

<h2 id="too-good">Why Do Buy-Before-You-Sell Programs Sound Too Good to Be True?</h2>

<p>Because the pitch skips the hard part. The pitch is "move once, skip showings, make a stronger offer." All three can be true. What gets left out is who pays for that convenience (you do), what has to go right for it to work (approval, appraisal, and the sale), and what happens if it doesn't.</p>

<p>Here's how it works when it goes well. A program advances part of your current home's equity so you can make a down payment on the next one. Your offer doesn't depend on selling first, so the seller treats you like a buyer with no house to sell. You close and move in. Then your old home is refreshed, cleaned, and staged, listed, and sold, and the advance is repaid from your proceeds. See <a href="/buy-before-you-sell">how buying before you sell works, step by step</a>.</p>

<p>None of that is a gimmick. It's a financing product with a price, and like any financing product, the details matter more than the headline.</p>

<h2 id="fees">Catch #1: What Do the Fees Really Cost You?</h2>

<p>Program fees usually run about 2–3% of your home's sale price, and up to 5% with some programs. On a $710,000 home, 2–3% is $14,200 to $21,300. That's the number people fixate on, and they're right to look at it closely.</p>

<p>Watch for a minimum fee, too. Some programs charge a flat floor (one sets it at $9,000), so on a lower-priced home the fee works out to a bigger share of the price than the advertised percentage.</p>

<p>But the fee isn't the whole picture. If you sell first instead, you'll probably pay for things the program lets you skip: rent while you find the next house, a second move, and a storage unit for everything that won't fit in the rental. The honest question isn't "is the fee big?" It's "how much of the fee do those savings cover, and is the rest worth it to us?"</p>

<p>Two things aren't in that math, and both can tip it. First, an offer that doesn't depend on selling your home is the kind sellers prefer, which can mean better price or terms on the next house, or simply winning a house you'd otherwise lose. Second, once you've moved out, your old home can be refreshed, cleaned, and staged into its best version, which is hard to pull off while a family lives there. <h2 id="approval">Catch #2: Could It Affect Your Loan Approval?</h2>

<p>This is the catch that surprises people most. Buying first takes two approvals: one for the advance and one for the new mortgage. Getting one doesn't guarantee the other.</p>

<p>The sticking point is usually your <a href="https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-debt-to-income-ratio-en-1791/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">debt-to-income ratio</a>, which lenders use to decide how much new debt you can handle. Some equity advance programs are set up so your current mortgage doesn't count against you when you qualify for the new loan. That's the main reason buying first works for families who could never carry two mortgages on paper. But that treatment varies by program and lender. If you borrow against your current home another way, with a HELOC or a short-term bridge loan, that payment usually does count.</p>

<p>The appraisal matters too. How much a program will advance depends on what your current home appraises for. If it comes in low, the advance shrinks, and so does your down payment. Some programs also exclude certain loan types, such as FHA loans, on the new home.</p>

<p>How to protect yourself: bring the lender in on day one, get approved for both the advance and the new mortgage before you shop, and ask in writing how your current mortgage and the advance will be counted.</p>

<h2 id="guaranteed">Catch #3: Is a "Guaranteed" Offer Really Guaranteed?</h2>

<p>Some programs pair the buy-first piece with a guaranteed or cash offer on your current home, a floor price you can fall back on. That can feel like a safety net. It also tends to be the most expensive line in the whole move.</p>

<p>Cash and guaranteed offers typically come in around 80–85% of market value. On a $710,000 home, 15–20% is $106,500 to $142,000 of equity. A "guaranteed" number can also change: many programs make a preliminary offer and adjust it after an inspection. That's where a lot of the "lowball" and "bait and switch" complaints come from.</p>

<p>A floor price is worth knowing about, especially if certainty matters more to you than top dollar. Just compare it to what a well-priced, well-prepared listing would net you before you treat it as the plan. For most families with solid equity, the guaranteed offer belongs in the comparison as a backstop, not the first choice.</p>

<h2 id="pricing">Catch #4: How Does Jacob Stark Keep the Old House From Sitting?</h2>

<p>Buying first doesn't remove the need to sell well. It raises the stakes. The advance is repaid from your sale proceeds, and most programs have a deadline. Every month the old home sits, you're paying its taxes, insurance, and utilities, plus any mortgage payments the advance doesn't cover. An overpriced listing hurts twice.</p>

<p>That's why Jacob Stark prices to sell in the first few weeks instead of testing the market. His listings average 19 days on market and 101.8% of list price. And because the house is refreshed, cleaned, and staged before it goes live, it launches as its best version instead of working around a family's daily life.</p>

<p>If showings are light at the three-week mark, the plan adjusts rather than waiting. (For what tends to work and what doesn't when a listing stalls, see <a href="/blog/price-reductions-highlands-ranch-what-works-2026">why Highlands Ranch price cuts often don't save a listing</a> and <a href="/blog/parker-move-up-listings-stall-pricing-strategy-2026">why Parker move-up listings stall</a>.) Not sure what's worth fixing before you list? Start with <a href="/what-to-fix-before-selling">what to fix before selling, and what to skip</a>.</p>

<h2 id="falls-through">Catch #5: What If the Program Falls Through Late?</h2>

<p>The worst stories about these programs share a pattern: something failed near the finish line. Funding didn't arrive on time. An approval was conditional on something nobody explained. The old house didn't sell by the deadline, and the backup terms weren't what the family expected.</p>

<p>Programs handle the deadline differently. Some buy your home for the amount needed to pay off your loans and pass along any upside when it resells, minus costs. Others start charging fees or interest. Either way, you want to know before you sign, not when you're under the wire.</p>

<p>How to protect yourself: read the backup terms, get full approval (not a pre-qualification) before you write an offer, keep the lender and title company in the loop from the start, and work with someone who answers the phone when it matters. A lot of the frustration in these reviews comes from families who felt left wondering at the worst possible moment.</p>

<h2 id="worth-it">When Is Buying First Clearly Worth It?</h2>

<p>After all those catches, buying first is still the right move for a lot of South Denver Metro families. It tends to be worth it when:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>You have solid equity</strong>, usually about 20% or more. See <a href="/blog/equity-to-move-up-highlands-ranch">how much equity you need to move up</a>, or run your numbers in the <a href="/tools/home-equity-calculator">home equity calculator</a>.</li>
<li><strong>The homes you want draw competing offers</strong>, so an offer that doesn't depend on your sale actually wins you something.</li>
<li><strong>Moving twice with kids is a dealbreaker</strong>, or keeping the house show-ready for weeks just isn't realistic.</li>
<li><strong>Your home would show much better</strong> refreshed and staged than it does lived-in.</li>
</ul>

<p>It probably isn't for you if your equity is thin, you need every dollar of it for the next down payment, or keeping costs as low as possible matters more than convenience. In those cases, selling first is often the smarter call, and there's nothing wrong with that.</p>

<h2 id="questions">Which 6 Questions Does Jacob Stark Tell Families to Ask Before Signing?</h2>

<p>Whichever program or agent you use, get clear answers to these in writing before you sign anything:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>What's the total fee, in dollars, on my home?</strong> Not a percentage range. A number.</li>
<li><strong>How will my current mortgage and the advance count when I qualify for the new loan?</strong></li>
<li><strong>What happens if my home appraises lower than expected?</strong></li>
<li><strong>What's the deadline to sell, and exactly what happens if I miss it?</strong></li>
<li><strong>Can any offer or advance amount change after an inspection, and why?</strong></li>
<li><strong>What would I net on every path, side by side?</strong> That means buying first, selling first, and a guaranteed offer, all compared on the same page.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="faq">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<h3>Are buy-before-you-sell programs worth the fee?</h3>
<p>Sometimes. Program fees usually run about 2–3% of your home's sale price, and up to 5% with some programs. The rent, extra move, and storage they replace typically cover part of that, not all of it, so the rest is what you pay for moving once, skipping showings, and making an offer that doesn't depend on selling first. They tend to be worth it when you have solid equity, the homes you want draw competing offers, and moving twice with kids is a dealbreaker.</p>

<h3>Does an equity advance affect my debt-to-income ratio?</h3>
<p>It depends on the program and the lender. Some programs are set up so your current mortgage doesn't count against you when you qualify for the new loan, while a HELOC or bridge loan payment usually does count. Ask the program and your lender, in writing, how the advance and your current mortgage will be treated before you start shopping.</p>

<h3>What happens if my old home doesn't sell in time?</h3>
<p>Each program has a deadline, often around four months. Some programs then buy your home for the amount needed to pay off your loans and pass along any upside when it resells, minus costs. Others start charging fees or interest. Read the backup terms before you sign, and price the home to sell well before the deadline.</p>

<h3>Can I use a buy-before-you-sell program with an FHA loan?</h3>
<p>Sometimes, but check first. Some programs don't allow an FHA loan on the new home, and others limit which loan types they work with. Ask the program and your lender which loan types qualify before you start shopping, so you know what down payment you'll actually need.</p>
