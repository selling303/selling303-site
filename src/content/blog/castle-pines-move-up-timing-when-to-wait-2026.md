<p>Most Castle Pines move-up sellers walk into the decision the same way. They have real equity. They have a clear sense that the current home is the wrong size or wrong layout for the next chapter. And they have a vague feeling that "the timing should be better." That feeling is usually built on three quiet assumptions: prices will keep rising, rates will keep falling, and the right replacement home will be obviously available. None of the three is supported by the April 2026 data. This post is for the <a href="/move-up-sellers">move-up seller</a> in <a href="/neighborhoods/castle-pines">Castle Pines</a> who is trying to decide whether 2026 is the year. It also names the reasons that actually justify waiting until 2027 or 2028.</p>

<p>The argument is narrower than "wait or sell." Most Castle Pines sellers should not wait, and the math on the page below shows why. But a meaningful minority should wait — for reasons that are specific, non-price, and testable in advance. Jacob Stark works with both groups, and the dividing line between them is rarely about how the broader Castle Pines market is doing. It is about a tax threshold, a replacement-side inventory gap, or a tier-jump that today's listing market does not yet support. The job of this post is to help you tell which side of that line you are on, before you list the home or before you decide to wait.</p>

<h2 id="why-waiting-question">Why Is Waiting Even a Question in Castle Pines?</h2>

<p>The reflex to wait is anchored in two real features of the Castle Pines market. First, the inventory is thin. Castle Pines and Castle Pines North combined carried 108 active residential listings in April 2026. The month also held 23 closings, 28 pending, 5 expired, and 6 withdrawn or coming soon. That is 164 listings tracked in total, per the REcolorado MLS Market Analysis Summary. That mix means there is rarely a glut on either side of the transaction. A move-up seller looking at the next home often sees a short list of fits, which makes waiting feel safer than acting.</p>

<p>Second, the price level is high enough that small percentage moves matter in absolute dollars. The April 2026 median close was $1,030,000. Closings ranged from $479,990 in the entry-tier paired-home product to $3,795,000 in the established custom-home enclaves. At those price points, a 2 percent shift up or down is $20,000 to $75,000. That is enough to make waiting feel like a real decision instead of a rounding error. The instinct is rational. What is not rational is assuming that 2 percent will move the way you want.</p>

<p>The third piece of context is the macro market. The <a href="https://www.dmarealtors.com/market-trends" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Denver Metro Association of Realtors</a> (DMAR) April 2026 Market Trends Report shows a median close price of $605,000 across the metro. The same metric was $604,000 in April 2025 and $602,000 in April 2024. Three consecutive Aprils inside a $3,000 band on a $600,000-plus median is the definition of a flat market. Castle Pines does not live independently of that. The same close-to-list discipline, the same buyer behavior, the same inventory rhythm. If the question is "will the Castle Pines market do something different in 2027 than it is doing now," the metro data does not support a confident yes.</p>

The line items: roughly $7,000 in Douglas County property tax, $3,500 in insurance, and $2,500 to $3,500 in HOA depending on sub-association. Add $15,000 to $18,000 for maintenance, mortgage interest on whatever balance remains, and utilities differential. Two years of waiting costs about $60,000 even before any change in the sale price.</p>

<h2 id="section-121">When Does Section 121 Tax Timing Make Waiting the Right Call?</h2>

<p>Section 121 of the Internal Revenue Code is the single most powerful timing lever available to a Castle Pines move-up seller, and it is also the most often overlooked. The rule excludes up to $250,000 of capital gain for a single filer and up to $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly, provided you have owned and used the home as your primary residence for at least two of the five years before the sale. Federal long-term capital gains tax runs 15 to 20 percent depending on income, plus Colorado state tax on the non-excluded portion, so at the Castle Pines price level the excluded gain can represent $40,000 to $100,000 or more of saved tax. That is real money against a move-up — and on the chart above, it can flip the math on its own.</p>

<p>The waiting case is specific. If you bought your Castle Pines home within the last two years, selling now means none of your gain qualifies for the exclusion. Wait until you cross the two-of-five threshold, and the exclusion opens. If a death, divorce, military deployment, or long absence has interrupted the use test, the same logic applies with different details. The Internal Revenue Service publishes the partial-exclusion exceptions in its <a href="https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc701" target="_blank" rel="noopener">primary residence sale topic</a>. A CPA can map your specific facts to those exceptions. Jacob Stark works with Castle Pines sellers' tax advisors at the listing-prep stage exactly to surface this kind of constraint before the home goes on market. The right answer in a Section 121 proximity case is often to wait six to eighteen months, not to list immediately.</p>

<p>Two cautions. First, the Section 121 exclusion is not new gain — it shields gain you have already earned. So waiting only helps if the gain at risk is meaningful relative to your move-up math. A Castle Pines seller with a $200,000 gain has less exposure than a long-tenured owner with an $800,000 gain — but a long-tenured owner with $800,000 of gain is also already past the threshold and gets nothing from waiting. Second, federal and state tax law can change. A timing decision built on today's exclusion math should be confirmed before you act, not just before you list.</p>

<h2 id="tier-replacement">When Does Waiting for the Next Tier or the Right Replacement Pay Off?</h2>

<p>The second real reason to wait is structural inventory. Castle Pines had 108 active residential listings in April 2026, with the price range spread from $479,990 entry-tier paired homes up to $4,500,000 estates. Inside that distribution, certain product types are scarce in any given month. Examples: single-level patio homes, age-targeted patio communities, custom-built lots in the Village at Castle Pines, and specific square-footage and lot-size combinations in BackCountry and the Canyons. If you are selling a Castle Pines home in order to move into one of those specific replacement types, the question is whether the replacement exists today.</p>

<p>The honest test is whether you can name the replacement. "We want a single-level home, low maintenance, under 3,000 square feet, in Castle Pines North or the Hearth Collection" is a real spec. You can scan today's 108 active listings and see whether two or three fit. If they do not, you have two options. Widen the replacement criteria, or wait for the next twelve months of new construction and resale to surface what you want. Either is defensible. What is not defensible is "we will know it when we see it" combined with a refusal to commit to today's inventory. That is a preference for waiting dressed up as a strategy.</p>

<p>The tier-jump version of the same argument runs through equity. Take a Castle Pines seller who is $40,000 short of the equity needed to land cleanly in the next price tier — say, moving from a $1.1 million home into a $1.8 million custom. That seller has a real reason to wait if the gap closes over the next eighteen months. The gap can close via payoff of principal, an inheritance, or a planned bonus. Waiting is not the strategy in that case. Closing the gap is the strategy, and waiting is the side effect. Same outcome, different framing — and the framing matters because it tells you what you are actually waiting for.</p>

<h2 id="wrong-reasons">What Are the Wrong Reasons to Wait in Castle Pines?</h2>

<p>Three reasons come up in nearly every Castle Pines move-up conversation, and the April 2026 data does not support any of them. Naming them is the fastest way to test whether you have a real timing reason or a story.</p>

<p>The first is "Castle Pines will appreciate." The market closed at a $1,030,000 median in April 2026 on a 97 percent close-to-original-list ratio, with 5 expired listings against 23 closings. That is a market that is well-functioning at current pricing, not a market with pent-up upward pressure. Three Aprils of essentially flat metro pricing per DMAR confirm the pattern is not local noise. There is no reliable basis to assume 2027 or 2028 will be materially different, and the chart above shows that even a generous 3 percent annual gain produces only $12,000 of upside on a $1.3 million sale.</p>

<p>The second is "rates will come down." Mortgage rates affect what your buyers can afford and what you can afford on the replacement side. If both move together, the spread between your sale price and your purchase price does not change. The cases where rate changes actually move the move-up math are narrow — a seller paying cash, or a seller buying into a substantially smaller home — and they rarely describe the typical Castle Pines move-up. The <a href="https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey</a> publishes the current 30-year fixed rate weekly, and it is worth tracking, but it is not by itself a reason to delay a Castle Pines sale.</p>

<p>The third is "the spring market will be stronger." The 2026 spring market in Castle Pines closed 23 homes in April at a 97 percent close-to-original-list ratio with an 18-day median time in MLS. That is a strong, well-functioning seller market today. Holding for next spring assumes a future spring will outperform this one, against three Aprils of flat metro data and a Castle Pines market that already prices and sells well. It is the same waiting bet as "prices will rise," dressed in seasonal language.</p>

<p>None of these is a fatal mistake on its own. The fatal mistake is letting one of them carry a decision that should be made on the real reasons — tax, tier, replacement. Jacob Stark's rule with Castle Pines sellers is that holding the home is a strategy when the seller can name what they are waiting for. If the answer is a date on the calendar with no specific event behind it, the answer is to list.</p>

<h2 id="faq">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<h3>Will Castle Pines home prices rise enough in 2027 or 2028 to make waiting worth it?</h3>

<p>There is no reliable basis to assume that. Castle Pines and Castle Pines North closed at a $1,030,000 median in April 2026 on 23 sales, and the broader Denver Metro median has held within $602,000 to $605,000 across April 2024, April 2025, and April 2026 per the DMAR April 2026 Market Trends Report. A flat market is what the data actually shows. Waiting two years against carrying costs of $25,000 to $40,000 per year is a real loss in any flat or softening scenario, and even a 3 percent annual gain barely offsets the wait. Price is not a good reason to hold a Castle Pines move-up.</p>

<h3>When does Section 121 tax timing make waiting the right call in Castle Pines?</h3>

<p>Section 121 of the Internal Revenue Code excludes up to $250,000 of capital gain for a single filer and $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly. The exclusion applies when you have owned and used the home as your primary residence for at least two of the five years before the sale. Two situations open the timing case. The first is buying your Castle Pines home within the last two years. The second is a death, divorce, or extended absence that has interrupted the use test. In either case, waiting until you clear the threshold can save federal long-term capital gains tax of 15 to 20 percent plus Colorado state tax on the excluded portion. Confirm the math with a CPA before treating it as a timing decision.</p>

<h3>Is it worth waiting two years for Castle Pines replacement inventory to improve?</h3>

<p>Sometimes yes. Castle Pines had only 108 active residential listings in April 2026 against 23 closings, and 5 expired listings sat for a 63-day median before coming off the market. If the next home is a specific single-level patio home, a Castle Pines North custom build, or a Village at Castle Pines lot type that rarely trades, waiting for replacement inventory to cycle is a defensible reason to hold. The honest test is whether you can name the actual replacement, not whether you are vaguely waiting for the market to feel right.</p>
