<p>If your home is listed in <a href="/neighborhoods/highlands-ranch">Highlands Ranch</a>, <a href="/neighborhoods/parker">Parker</a>, <a href="/neighborhoods/littleton">Littleton</a>, or anywhere else in the South Denver Metro right now, you've probably had the conversation. Showings are slower. Feedback mentions "payment." Someone suggests another price cut.</p>

<p>Before you take it, compare the cut with a seller-paid 2-1 buydown. Spend the same dollars both ways and see what the buyer actually feels. On a typical home, the buydown lowers the buyer's payment about ten times as much in the first year. That gap is why it belongs in every <a href="/move-up-sellers">move-up seller strategy</a> this fall.</p>

<p>The catch is attention. A price change tells every portal and saved search that something changed. A buydown doesn't. Below is the math, how to get a buydown noticed, and a calculator you can run on your own numbers.</p>

<h2 id="why-now">Why Are South Denver Metro Sellers Facing This Choice Right Now?</h2>

<p>Because the market shifted in September, and the rate shift came on top of it. Here's what the <a href="https://www.dmarealtors.com/market-trends" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DMAR September 2026 Market Trends Report</a> showed for the Denver Metro:</p>

<ul>
<li>2,849 closings, the fewest for any September in data back to 2008.</li>
<li>13,567 active listings, the most for a September since 2011.</li>
<li>4.76 months of inventory overall.</li>
</ul>

<p>Mortgage rates moved at the same time. <a href="https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Freddie Mac's weekly average</a> for a 30-year fixed loan rose from 6.67% in mid-August to 7.28% on October 1. On a $520,000 loan, that adds about $213 a month in principal and interest. A buyer who was comfortable in August is looking at a bigger payment for the same house.</p>

<p>Sellers have mostly answered with price cuts. REcolorado MLS data for nine South Denver Metro cities shows 53% to 64% of active listings had already cut their price by early October. In Littleton, 62% of 861 active listings had a cut. In Parker, 60% of 576 had one. If you're about to cut again, you're joining a crowd. The <a href="/blog/price-reductions-highlands-ranch-what-works-2026">Highlands Ranch price-reduction data</a> shows why a second cut often doesn't fix a stalled listing.</p>

<h2 id="what-is-buydown">What Is a 2-1 Buydown, and Who Pays for It?</h2>

<p>A 2-1 buydown is prepaid interest that lowers the buyer's rate for two years. The rate drops 2 points in year one and 1 point in year two. In year three, it returns to the full note rate for the life of the loan.</p>

<p>The seller can fund it as a credit at closing. The money sits in a buydown account, and the lender draws from it each month to cover the difference in payment.</p>

<p>A few rules come from the buyer's loan program, not from you:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>The buyer still qualifies at the full rate.</strong> <a href="https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b2-1.4-04/temporary-interest-rate-buydowns" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Fannie Mae's temporary buydown rules</a> require lenders to qualify the buyer at the note rate. A buydown makes the payment easier to live with. It doesn't stretch what the buyer can borrow.</li>
<li><strong>Concession caps apply.</strong> Under <a href="https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.1-02/interested-party-contributions-ipcs" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Fannie Mae's contribution limits</a>, a seller can contribute 3% of the price when the buyer puts less than 10% down. The cap is 6% with 10% to 25% down and 9% with more than 25% down. FHA allows 6%, and VA caps concessions at 4%.</li>
<li><strong>Primary homes and second homes only.</strong> Investor loans aren't eligible for a temporary buydown.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="run-the-math">How Does Jacob Stark Compare a Price Cut and a Buydown on the Same Home?</h2>

<p>Take a $650,000 home. The buyer puts 20% down on a $520,000 loan at 7.28%. Their principal and interest payment is $3,558 a month.</p>

<p>A 2-1 buydown on that loan costs about $12,300, or 1.9% of the price. Here's what that money does each way:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>As a 2-1 buydown:</strong> the payment drops to $2,881 in year one, a savings of $677 a month. In year two it's $3,212, a savings of $346 a month. Total savings over two years come to the full $12,300.</li>
<li><strong>As a $12,300 price cut:</strong> the price drops to about $637,700. The payment drops to $3,491, a savings of $67 a month. The buyer also brings about $2,450 less for the down payment.</li>
</ul>

<p>This is an example, not a quote. Your numbers depend on the buyer's loan, rate, and down payment, so talk with a lender about your specific situation before you offer anything.</p>

<p>The price cut does keep saving after year two. But at $67 a month, it takes about 15 years to match what the buydown delivers in the first two. Most buyers will sell or refinance before then.</p>

<p>There's one more difference for you as the seller. With a buydown, your recorded sale price stays at $650,000. That number becomes a comparable sale for your neighbors and for any appraisal on your next purchase. A price cut lowers it for good.</p>

<h2 id="attention">Why Doesn't a Buydown Get Noticed Like a Price Cut?</h2>

<p>Because nothing announces it. When you cut your price, Zillow, Realtor.com, and the MLS send alerts to buyers who saved your home or a matching search. A buydown leaves the list price alone, so none of those alerts go out.</p>

<p>A buydown is also harder to show off. Every listing site displays and tracks the price. There's no matching spot for a seller credit, so buyers only see it if your agent puts it in front of them.</p>

<p>The price-cut alert has limits, though. A <a href="https://www.housingwire.com/articles/49168-this-is-why-dropping-your-list-price-can-earn-you-a-scarlet-letter/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Redfin study of 1.2 million listings</a> from 2018 and 2019 found the bump lasts about a day. A home that drew 100 views on day one was down to about 17 a day by day 30. A price cut lifted it to 29 views for one day. The next day it was back near 18.</p>

<p>So each tool has one job. A price cut buys a short burst of new attention. A buydown turns an interested buyer into an offer.</p>

<h2 id="which-one">Which One Fits Your Listing?</h2>

<p>Start with your showings. They tell you whether you have an attention problem, a payment problem, or both.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Tours, but no offers.</strong> Buyers have found the house and liked it enough to visit. Attention isn't the problem. The payment is. Offer the buydown, and take it straight to the agents and buyers who've already toured.</li>
<li><strong>Few or no tours.</strong> Buyers are passing on the listing page, and most of them search by price range. You need attention, and only a price cut creates it. This is also the point to look at photos, condition, and competing listings, which the <a href="/blog/why-house-not-selling-denver">guide to why houses don't sell in Denver</a> walks through.</li>
<li><strong>Slow tours, and the few buyers mention payment.</strong> This is where many stalled South Denver Metro listings sit this fall. Split the budget.</li>
</ul>

<p>Here's how a split works. Any price change, even a small one, sends the alerts. The size of the cut decides who gets them. A cut that stays in the same range, like $715,000 to $705,000, reaches buyers who already saved the home or a search that includes it. A cut that crosses a common search cutoff, like $715,000 to $699,000, also reaches every buyer whose search stops at $700,000.</p>

<p>Then make the buydown impossible to miss. The alert brings buyers back to your listing, so the credit has to be the first thing they see. Put it in the first line of the description, and in the first photos as a simple graphic if your MLS allows it. Something as plain as "Seller will pay to lower your rate for two years" changes how a buyer reads the price.</p>

<p>If the split leaves less for the buydown, a 1-0 buydown is a cheaper option. It lowers the rate 1 point for year one only and costs about a third of a 2-1 on the same loan.</p>

<h2 id="catches">What Catches Does Jacob Stark Flag Before Offering a Buydown?</h2>

<p>A buydown is a strong tool, but it comes with fine print. Here's what to check before you put one in a counteroffer:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Write it as a credit.</strong> Phrase the offer as a seller credit the buyer may use toward a rate buydown or closing costs. The lender then sets it up in a way the loan program allows.</li>
<li><strong>It won't rescue a stretched buyer.</strong> The buyer qualifies at the full rate. If their debt-to-income ratio is the problem, a buydown won't solve it.</li>
<li><strong>The appraiser on your sale will see it.</strong> The buyer's appraiser reviews the purchase contract, so the credit shows up in that appraisal. Later, when your home is used as a comparable sale, an appraiser knows about the credit only if your agent reports it in the MLS at closing or the appraiser asks. Even then, they adjust only for how much the credit pushed the price up.</li>
<li><strong>Refinancing doesn't waste it.</strong> If rates fall and the buyer refinances early, unused buydown funds can go toward paying off the loan, per Fannie Mae's rules. That's a real selling point for nervous buyers.</li>
<li><strong>Put it where buyers look.</strong> The portals won't announce a buydown for you. Lead the description with it, show it in the first photos if your MLS allows, tell the agents who have shown the home, and leave a one-page payment comparison at showings.</li>
</ul>

<p>Selling one home to buy the next adds a layer. Every dollar of concession comes out of the equity you're carrying into your next purchase. <h2 id="faq">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<h3>Is a 2-1 buydown better than a price reduction?</h3>
<p>For the buyer's monthly payment, usually yes. On a $650,000 South Denver Metro home with 20% down at 7.28%, about $12,300 spent on a 2-1 buydown lowers the payment $677 a month in year one and $346 in year two. The same $12,300 taken off the price lowers it about $67 a month. A price reduction still matters, because it's the only change that sends alerts to buyers.</p>

<h3>Who pays for a 2-1 buydown when selling a house in Colorado?</h3>
<p>The seller can pay for it as a credit at closing. The buyer's lender applies the credit to a temporary buydown. The loan program caps the amount. For a conventional loan on a primary home, the cap is 3%, 6%, or 9% of the price, depending on the buyer's down payment.</p>

<h3>Will buyers see a seller-paid buydown on Zillow or in their saved searches?</h3>
<p>Not on its own. Any price change sends alerts to buyers who saved your home or a matching search. A buydown doesn't change the list price, so it sends none. Pair a price reduction with a buydown credit, and lead the description and first photos with the credit so buyers see it when the alert brings them back.</p>
