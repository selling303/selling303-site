<h2 id="structures">Which Coordination Structure Fits Which Centennial Seller?</h2>

<p>Three coordination structures reliably work for a Centennial dual transaction in 2026. The right one depends on the replacement-market velocity, the seller's tolerance for temporary housing, and the rate environment.</p>

<p><strong>Sell first, then write a contingent purchase.</strong> Accept the Centennial offer first. Then submit a replacement-home offer contingent on closing the Centennial sale by a stated date. This is the cleanest financial structure. No bridge debt. No carrying-cost overlap. It works best when the next market is cooperative on contingencies. That means slower-moving suburbs or higher-tier price bands where sellers have time to wait. It works less well inside the same $650K to $1M tier the seller just left. The buyer pool is competitive and contingencies often get out-bid.</p>

<p><strong>Sell first with a seller rent-back.</strong> Accept the Centennial offer with a 15 to 30 day post-closing rent-back. The seller keeps paid-for occupancy in the Centennial home while the next search and closing complete. This structure works in nearly any offer scenario. The rent-back is part of the offer terms, not the financing. The trade-off: the rent-back has to be in the offer at acceptance, not added later. And it sits inside the buyer's mortgage approval window.</p>

<p><strong>HELOC-backed bridge.</strong> Draw a home equity line of credit on the Centennial home before listing. Use the HELOC to close on the replacement first. Then sell the Centennial home and pay off the HELOC. This is the most expensive coordination structure. HELOC rates run 9 to 11 percent in 2026 per <a href="https://www.freddiemac.com/pmms" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Freddie Mac PMMS</a> context for second-position lending. But the structure removes the timing mismatch. Use it when the replacement is in a tight market where contingencies will lose. The seller has to absorb 30 to 90 days of HELOC carrying cost. And the Centennial home has to be well-positioned for a fast sale. The 12-day median time in MLS environment is exactly that.</p>

The window collapses if the Centennial home does not contract inside the first 12 to 21 days.</p>

<h2 id="sequence">How Do You Stage the Seven Milestones in the Right Order?</h2>

<p>The question is whether to pre-stage everything before the listing goes live, or to handle each milestone as it arrives. The data answers it. A Centennial home that goes live with no replacement short list and no drafted coordination structure runs the 12-day median DIM clock against an unprepared seller. By Day 8, an offer is in hand. By Day 14, the offer is signed. By Day 35, the closing is on the calendar. And the seller has not yet identified the next home.</p>

<p>The pre-staged sequence (Steps 1 through 4 complete before the listing goes live) inverts the pressure. The seller starts the listing window with a known coordination structure. Three to five replacement candidates are already in active showing rotation. The lender re-qualification at the next price point is already in hand. The contingency offer is pre-drafted and ready to submit. When a Centennial offer arrives on Day 8, the seller signs the offer that fits the coordination structure. The replacement-home offer goes out the same week. The dual closing lands in the planned 30 to 45 day window. The timing was decided in advance, not improvised under contract.</p>

<h2 id="mistakes">What Goes Wrong When a Milestone Gets Skipped?</h2>

<p>Three specific failure patterns show up repeatedly. The first is sequencing the replacement search after the listing offer is signed. By the time the seller has an offer in hand, the closing date is 28 to 35 days out. The replacement search has not started. Inventory has turned over. The seller writes a contingent offer under time pressure. The contingency gets declined. The fix is pre-staging Steps 1 through 4 before the listing is active.</p>

<p>The second is mispricing the listing to the active comp set rather than the closed comp set. Active comps show what sellers are asking. Closed comps show what buyers are paying. The 98 percent median CP/OLP ratio in April 2026 means most Centennial listings closed near asking. But that ratio only applies to listings that contracted. The 29 listings that failed in April were mostly anchored to active comps. Pricing to active comps is the most common reason a dual-transaction window collapses before it starts. A related diagnostic for stale listings is covered in <a href="/blog/why-centennial-home-not-getting-offers">why your Centennial home isn't getting offers</a>.</p>

<p>The third is accepting the highest gross offer without checking timeline fit. The strongest offer for a dual-transaction seller is the one whose closing date aligns with the next home. It should include rent-back terms if needed. And it should carry financing that will not slip. The highest gross offer with a misaligned closing date costs more than it adds. Jacob Stark coaches Centennial move-up sellers to grade offers on four dimensions, not just price — net proceeds, closing date alignment, financing strength, and contingency posture.</p>

<h2 id="faq">Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<h3>What is a sell-first contingent purchase in Colorado?</h3>
<p>A sell-first contingent purchase is a replacement-home offer that is contingent on closing the seller's existing home by a stated date. The Colorado Real Estate Commission contract uses standard contingency language. The replacement-home offer becomes non-binding if the existing-home sale does not close on time. The structure works best when the replacement seller has flexibility on closing. Slower-moving suburbs, higher price bands, and estate or relocation sellers are good fits. A 30 to 45 day extension is not a deal-breaker for them.</p>

<h3>How much rent-back should a Centennial seller negotiate?</h3>
<p>Most rent-back terms in Centennial 2026 run 15 to 30 days post-closing. The rent-back rate is typically the buyer's PITI prorated daily. The seller pays roughly what the buyer's housing cost is for those days. Longer rent-backs of 60 to 90 days are possible. But they are hard to negotiate in a fast market where the buyer wants to move in. Anything beyond 30 days should be negotiated into the offer at acceptance, not added after the inspection objection period.</p>

<h3>Do Centennial buyers accept contingent offers in a fast market?</h3>
<p>It depends on the price band. In the $650K to $1M tier, the median time in MLS is 12 days. A contingent offer from a Centennial seller usually loses to a clean offer. The exception: when the gross price meaningfully exceeds the competing offer. In slower-moving replacement markets, contingent offers fare better. Examples: higher-tier Centennial luxury, secondary Arapahoe County suburbs, or older-stock neighborhoods with longer DIM. Pre-staging the lender re-qualification at the replacement price point sharply improves contingent-offer competitiveness regardless of band.</p>
